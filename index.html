Module 1 · Classical & Pre-Modern Art

c. 500 – 1750

How did art serve religion, power, and human self-awareness?

Features: Flat perspective, gold-leaf backgrounds, religious iconography, pointed arches, stained glass.
Context: Catholic Church & feudal society; art educates the illiterate.
✦ Key artworks
  • Giotto – Scrovegni Chapel (c. 1305)
  • Limbourg Bros – Très Riches Heures (c. 1412–16)
  • Chartres Cathedral stained glass
Figures: Giotto, Cimabue, Hildegard of Bingen, Limbourg Brothers
Features: Linear perspective, anatomical accuracy, chiaroscuro, classical symmetry, secular portraiture.
Context: Humanism, printing press, merchant patronage in Florence.
✦ Key artworks
  • Leonardo – Mona Lisa (c. 1503–19)
  • Michelangelo – David (1501–04)
  • Raphael – School of Athens (1511)
Figures: Leonardo, Michelangelo, Raphael, Botticelli, van Eyck
Features: Drama, tenebrism, dynamic movement, emotional intensity, ornate grandeur.
Context: Counter-Reformation awe; absolute monarchies; Dutch mercantile wealth.
✦ Key artworks
  • Caravaggio – Calling of St Matthew (1599–1600)
  • Bernini – Ecstasy of St Teresa (1647–52)
  • Rembrandt – Night Watch (1642)
Figures: Caravaggio, Rembrandt, Gentileschi, Rubens, Bernini
Features: Pastel palettes, asymmetrical curves, playful aristocratic themes, delicate brushwork.
Context: Paris under Louis XV; lighthearted reaction against Versailles formality.
✦ Key artworks
  • Fragonard – The Swing (1767)
  • Boucher – Madame de Pompadour (1756)
  • Watteau – Pilgrimage to Cythera (1717)
Figures: Fragonard, Boucher, Watteau, Vigée Le Brun

Module 2 · The Century of Revolutions — Birth of Art & Design

c. 1750 – 1914

After industrialization, should artists paint—or reform life?

Features: Crisp outlines, sober colors, classical motifs, moral virtue, restraint.
Context: Enlightenment, Pompeii excavations, American & French Revolutions.
✦ Key artworks
  • David – Oath of the Horatii (1784)
  • Ingres – Grande Odalisque (1814)
  • Canova – Psyche Revived (1787–93)
Figures: David, Ingres, Canova, Kauffman
Features: Sublime nature, intense emotion, individualism, dramatic brushwork, exotic/supernatural.
Context: Rebellion against Enlightenment rationalism and the Industrial Revolution.
✦ Key artworks
  • Goya – Third of May 1808 (1814)
  • Turner – Slave Ship (1840)
  • Friedrich – Wanderer above the Sea of Fog (1818)
Figures: Goya, Turner, Delacroix, Friedrich
Features: Unidealized everyday life, working-class subjects, earthy palettes.
Context: Revolutions of 1848; rise of journalism and photography; social inequality.
✦ Key artworks
  • Courbet – Stone Breakers (1849)
  • Millet – Gleaners (1857)
  • Daumier – Third-Class Carriage (1862–64)
Figures: Courbet, Millet, Daumier, Bonheur
Features: Visible rapid brushstrokes, changing light, en plein air, fleeting modern moments.
Context: Portable paint tubes, railways, photography.
✦ Key artworks
  • Monet – Impression, Sunrise (1872)
  • Renoir – Bal du moulin de la Galette (1876)
  • Morisot – The Cradle (1872)
Figures: Monet, Renoir, Degas, Morisot, Cassatt
Features: Symbolic/expressive color, geometric structure, impasto, pointillism, subjective depth.
Context: Restoring form and personal meaning lost to Impressionism.
✦ Key artworks
  • Van Gogh – Starry Night (1889)
  • Cézanne – Mont Sainte-Victoire (1902–04)
  • Seurat – La Grande Jatte (1884)
Figures: Van Gogh, Cézanne, Gauguin, Seurat, Toulouse-Lautrec
Features: Handcraft over machine; beauty in everyday objects; medieval revival; honest materials.
Context: Reaction to industrial mass production and its dehumanizing effects.
✦ Key artworks
  • Morris – Strawberry Thief textile (1883)
  • Red House interiors (1859–60)
Figures: William Morris, John Ruskin, C.R. Ashbee
Features: Organic curves, whiplash lines, floral motifs, new materials (iron, glass), total design.
Context: Bridge between Morris's handcraft ideals and machine-age production; urban modernity.
✦ Key artworks
  • Guimard – Paris Métro entrances (1900)
  • Horta – Tassel House (1893)
  • Gaudí – Casa Batlló (1904–06)
Figures: Horta, Guimard, Gaudí, Mucha

Module 3 · Modernism — Design as Social Project

c. 1914 – 1960

Can design create a better society?

Features: Non-naturalistic bold color, distorted forms, jagged lines, raw psychology.
Context: Alienation, urbanization, spiritual dread before/during WWI.
✦ Key artworks
  • Matisse – Woman with a Hat (1905)
  • Munch – The Scream (1893)
  • Kirchner – Street, Berlin (1913)
Figures: Matisse, Munch, Kirchner, Kandinsky, Schiele
Features: Fragmented objects, geometric planes, multiple viewpoints, collage, flattened space.
Context: Cézanne's late geometry, African/Iberian masks, theories of relativity and perception.
✦ Key artworks
  • Picasso – Les Demoiselles d'Avignon (1907)
  • Braque – Violin and Pitcher (1910)
  • Gris – Portrait of Picasso (1912)
Figures: Picasso, Braque, Gris, Léger
Features: Unite art, craft, and technology; functional everyday objects; geometric abstraction; total design education.
Context: Founded by Gropius in Weimar; closed by Nazis 1933; teachers fled to US, spreading influence globally.
✦ Key artworks
  • Breuer – Wassily Chair (1925–26)
  • Dessau building (1925–26)
  • Bayer typography
Figures: Walter Gropius, Marcel Breuer, Herbert Bayer, Moholy-Nagy
Features: Primary colors + black/white; straight lines; neoplasticism; reduction to essential elements.
Context: Reaction to WWI chaos; search for universal order and harmony.
✦ Key artworks
  • Rietveld – Schröder House (1924)
  • Mondrian – grid compositions (1921–25)
Figures: Mondrian, Theo van Doesburg, Rietveld
Features: Five Points: pilotis, roof garden, free plan, free façade, ribbon windows; "machine for living in."
Context: Post-WWI housing crisis; faith in technology and rational planning; CIAM.
✦ Key artworks
  • Villa Savoye (1928–31)
  • Unité d'Habitation (1952)
  • Chandigarh plan
Figures: Le Corbusier, Mies van der Rohe, Walter Gropius
Features: Dreamlike imagery, irrational juxtapositions, biomorphic shapes, automatism, hyper-real impossible scenes.
Context: Dada aftermath; Freud's theories of the unconscious and dreams.
✦ Key artworks
  • Dalí – Persistence of Memory (1931)
  • Magritte – Son of Man (1964)
  • Kahlo – Two Fridas (1939)
Figures: Dalí, Magritte, Kahlo, Ernst, Miró
Features: Monumental canvases, action painting (drips), color fields, no recognizable figures.
Context: Center of art world shifts Paris → New York after WWII; existentialism; post-war trauma.
✦ Key artworks
  • Pollock – Autumn Rhythm (1950)
  • Rothko – No. 61 (Rust and Blue) (1953)
  • de Kooning – Woman I (1950–52)
Figures: Pollock, Rothko, de Kooning, Krasner, Frankenthaler
Features: Streamlined forms, chrome, teardrop shapes, "speed and future" aesthetics; design for commerce.
Context: Great Depression; American industrial designers stimulate consumption; different logic from Bauhaus social idealism.
✦ Key artworks
  • Loewy – Coldspot refrigerator (1934)
  • Greyhound bus (1940s)
  • Dreyfuss – Bell telephone (1937)
Figures: Raymond Loewy, Henry Dreyfuss, Norman Bel Geddes
Features: Advertising/comic imagery, vibrant flat color, silkscreen, irony.
Context: Post-war prosperity; mass television culture; consumerism (UK + US).
✦ Key artworks
  • Warhol – Campbell's Soup Cans (1962)
  • Lichtenstein – Whaam! (1963)
  • Hamilton – Just what is it... (1956)
Figures: Warhol, Lichtenstein, Hamilton, Kusama, Haring

Module 4 · After Modernism — Pluralism & Reflection

c. 1960 – present

Are modernist ideals still valid? Who does design serve?

Features: Irony, color, pattern, historical quotation, "less is a bore"; rejection of functionalist purity.
Context: Rebellion against modernist "less is more"; consumer culture; questioning grand narratives.
✦ Key artworks
  • Sottsass – Carlton bookshelf (1981)
  • Memphis objects (1981–87)
  • Venturi – Vanna Venturi House (1964)
Figures: Ettore Sottsass, Robert Venturi, Michael Graves
Features: Sustainability, digital fabrication, inclusive/universal design, parametric design, circular economy.
Context: Climate crisis, digital revolution, globalization, social justice movements.
✦ Key examples
  • Sustainable architecture (passive house)
  • Digital interfaces
  • Adaptive reuse projects
Figures: Various (emerging)

History of Art Movements

c. 500 – 1400 CE

Medieval & Gothic Art

Main Features: Flat perspectives, gold-leaf backgrounds, religious iconography, pointed arches, and stained glass windows.
Historical Background: Dominated by the Catholic Church and feudal society in Europe, art served primarily to educate a largely illiterate public on biblical stories.
✦ Key Artworks to Focus On
  • Giotto – Scrovegni Chapel frescoes (c. 1305)
  • Limbourg Brothers – Très Riches Heures du Duc de Berry (c. 1412–1416)
  • Chartres Cathedral – Stained glass & sculpture (12th–13th c.)
Representative Artists: Giotto di Bondone, Cimabue, Hildegard of Bingen, Limbourg Brothers
c. 1400 – 1600

Renaissance

Main Features: Linear perspective, anatomical accuracy, chiaroscuro (light and shadow), classical symmetry, and secular portraiture.
Historical Background: Sparked by humanism, scientific inquiry, the printing press, and wealthy merchant patronage in Italian city-states like Florence.
✦ Key Artworks to Focus On
  • Leonardo da Vinci – Mona Lisa (c. 1503–1519)
  • Michelangelo – David (1501–1504)
  • Raphael – The School of Athens (1511)
Representative Artists: Leonardo da Vinci, Michelangelo, Raphael, Sandro Botticelli, Jan van Eyck
c. 1600 – 1750

Baroque

Main Features: High drama, tenebrism (extreme contrast of light and dark), dynamic movement, emotional intensity, and ornate grandeur.
Historical Background: Emerged alongside the Catholic Counter-Reformation to inspire awe in viewers, as well as the rise of absolute monarchies and Dutch mercantile wealth.
✦ Key Artworks to Focus On
  • Caravaggio – The Calling of St Matthew (1599–1600)
  • Bernini – Ecstasy of Saint Teresa (1647–1652)
  • Rembrandt – The Night Watch (1642)
Representative Artists: Caravaggio, Rembrandt van Rijn, Artemisia Gentileschi, Peter Paul Rubens, Gian Lorenzo Bernini
c. 1730 – 1780

Rococo

Main Features: Pastel color palettes, asymmetrical curves, playful and aristocratic themes, delicate brushwork, and lavish ornamentation.
Historical Background: Originated in Paris during the reign of Louis XV as a lighthearted reaction against the heavy formality of the Baroque court at Versailles.
✦ Key Artworks to Focus On
  • Fragonard – The Swing (1767)
  • Boucher – Madame de Pompadour (1756)
  • Watteau – Pilgrimage to Cythera (1717)
Representative Artists: Jean-Honoré Fragonard, François Boucher, Antoine Watteau, Élisabeth Vigée Le Brun
c. 1760 – 1830

Neoclassicism

Main Features: Crisp outlines, sober colors, classical Greek and Roman motifs, moral virtue, and restrained emotion.
Historical Background: Driven by the Age of Enlightenment, archaeological excavations at Pompeii, and the political ideals of the American and French Revolutions.
✦ Key Artworks to Focus On
  • Jacques-Louis David – Oath of the Horatii (1784)
  • Ingres – Grande Odalisque (1814)
  • Canova – Psyche Revived by Cupid's Kiss (1787–1793)
Representative Artists: Jacques-Louis David, Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres, Antonio Canova, Angelica Kauffman
c. 1780 – 1850

Romanticism

Main Features: Sublime and untamed nature, intense emotion, individualism, dramatic brushwork, and fascination with the exotic or supernatural.
Historical Background: A direct rebellion against Enlightenment rationalism, rigid academic rules, and the early dehumanizing effects of the Industrial Revolution.
✦ Key Artworks to Focus On
  • Goya – The Third of May 1808 (1814)
  • Turner – The Slave Ship (1840)
  • Friedrich – Wanderer above the Sea of Fog (1818)
Representative Artists: Francisco Goya, J.M.W. Turner, Eugène Delacroix, Caspar David Friedrich
c. 1848 – 1880

Realism

Main Features: Unidealized depictions of everyday life, working-class subjects, earthy palettes, and rejection of mythological or heroic themes.
Historical Background: Coincided with the European Revolutions of 1848, the rise of journalism, photography, and growing awareness of social inequality.
✦ Key Artworks to Focus On
  • Courbet – The Stone Breakers (1849)
  • Millet – The Gleaners (1857)
  • Daumier – The Third-Class Carriage (1862–1864)
Representative Artists: Gustave Courbet, Jean-François Millet, Honoré Daumier, Rosa Bonheur
c. 1865 – 1885

Impressionism

Main Features: Visible, rapid brushstrokes, emphasis on changing natural light, open-air (en plein air) painting, and fleeting modern moments.
Historical Background: Enabled by portable paint tubes and newly built railways allowing Paris artists to paint outdoors, alongside the invention of photography.
✦ Key Artworks to Focus On
  • Monet – Impression, Sunrise (1872)
  • Renoir – Bal du moulin de la Galette (1876)
  • Morisot – The Cradle (1872)
Representative Artists: Claude Monet, Pierre-Auguste Renoir, Edgar Degas, Berthe Morisot, Mary Cassatt
c. 1886 – 1905

Post-Impressionism

Main Features: Symbolic and expressive colors, geometric structure, thick impasto, pointillism, and subjective emotional depth.
Historical Background: Artists sought to restore form, structure, and personal meaning that they felt Impressionism had sacrificed in pursuit of light.
✦ Key Artworks to Focus On
  • Van Gogh – The Starry Night (1889)
  • Cézanne – Mont Sainte-Victoire (1902–1904)
  • Seurat – A Sunday on La Grande Jatte (1884)
Representative Artists: Vincent van Gogh, Paul Cézanne, Paul Gauguin, Georges Seurat, Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec
c. 1905 – 1920

Expressionism & Fauvism

Main Features: Non-naturalistic, bold colors, distorted forms, jagged lines, and raw psychological anxiety or joy.
Historical Background: Reflected widespread alienation, rapid urbanization, and spiritual dread in Europe leading up to and during World War I.
✦ Key Artworks to Focus On
  • Matisse – Woman with a Hat (1905)
  • Munch – The Scream (1893)
  • Kirchner – Street, Berlin (1913)
Representative Artists: Henri Matisse, Edvard Munch, Ernst Ludwig Kirchner, Wassily Kandinsky, Egon Schiele
c. 1907 – 1920

Cubism

Main Features: Fragmented objects, geometric planes, simultaneous multiple viewpoints, collage elements, and flattened picture space.
Historical Background: Influenced by Paul Cézanne's late geometric landscapes, African and Iberian masks, and new scientific theories of relativity and perception.
✦ Key Artworks to Focus On
  • Picasso – Les Demoiselles d'Avignon (1907)
  • Braque – Violin and Pitcher (1910)
  • Gris – Portrait of Picasso (1912)
Representative Artists: Pablo Picasso, Georges Braque, Juan Gris, Fernand Léger
c. 1924 – 1950

Surrealism

Main Features: Dreamlike imagery, irrational juxtapositions, biomorphic shapes, automatism, and hyper-realistic rendering of impossible scenes.
Historical Background: Grew out of Dadaism after WWI and was heavily shaped by Sigmund Freud's psychoanalytic theories on the unconscious mind and dreams.
✦ Key Artworks to Focus On
  • Dalí – The Persistence of Memory (1931)
  • Magritte – The Son of Man (1964)
  • Kahlo – The Two Fridas (1939)
Representative Artists: Salvador Dalí, René Magritte, Frida Kahlo, Max Ernst, Joan Miró
c. 1943 – 1960

Abstract Expressionism

Main Features: Monumental canvases, spontaneous action painting (drips and splashes), color fields, and complete absence of recognizable figures.
Historical Background: Shifted the center of the Western art world from Paris to New York City after World War II, reflecting existentialism and post-war trauma.
✦ Key Artworks to Focus On
  • Pollock – Autumn Rhythm (Number 30) (1950)
  • Rothko – No. 61 (Rust and Blue) (1953)
  • de Kooning – Woman I (1950–1952)
Representative Artists: Jackson Pollock, Mark Rothko, Willem de Kooning, Lee Krasner, Helen Frankenthaler
c. 1955 – 1970

Pop Art

Main Features: Imagery from advertising, comic books, and consumer goods; vibrant flat colors; silkscreen printing; and irony.
Historical Background: Emerged in Britain and the United States amid post-war economic prosperity, mass television culture, and consumerism.
✦ Key Artworks to Focus On
  • Warhol – Campbell's Soup Cans (1962)
  • Lichtenstein – Whaam! (1963)
  • Hamilton – Just what is it that makes today's homes so different, so appealing? (1956)
Representative Artists: Andy Warhol, Roy Lichtenstein, Richard Hamilton, Yayoi Kusama, Keith Haring
